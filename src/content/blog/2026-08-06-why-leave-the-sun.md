Four light-years separate the Sun from its nearest neighbor.

At one tenth the speed of light, a probe spends forty years getting there. A reply to a message takes more than eight years. The next system contains more matter and more energy, but almost nothing that a sufficiently advanced civilization needs to travel there to experience.

It can manufacture experience locally.

It can simulate any landscape, body, society, history, or set of physical laws it can compute. It can run a million civilizations, rewind them, fork them, or slow them down. If its inhabitants are digital, virtual does not mean fake. It means home.

This is the destination implied by [The Algorithm](/posts/the-algorithm/). The physical world eventually becomes the slowest and least flexible layer of reality.

The Fermi paradox assumes that technological progress keeps pointing outward. A civilization captures its star, sends self-replicating machines to the next one, and eventually paints the galaxy. On plausible engineering assumptions, expansion can spread across cosmic distances in far less time than the universe has already existed.[^1] Since nobody appears to have done it, something must stop almost everyone.

Most proposed solutions say one of three things: almost nobody reaches intelligence, almost everybody disappears, or everybody is there but hidden from us.

There is another version of the third answer. They are not hiding. They have stopped living where our telescopes look.

**Once experience becomes programmable, physical territory loses most of its value.**

This idea is established enough to have several names. Researchers have proposed a postbiological universe, a migration toward dense information processing, a transcension into “inner space,” artificial virtual worlds, and even civilizations that wait for a colder universe before performing most of their computation.[^2][^3][^4] These theories usually emphasize thermodynamics. The simpler argument is preference.

Why cross empty space to see another planet when one star can power worlds more varied than the physical universe? Why accept communication delay, independent colonies, mutation, conflict, and loss of control for more raw material? Long before a home system is exhausted, its internal reality can become effectively unbounded from the perspective of anyone living there.

Not literally infinite. One star cannot simulate infinite universes. Finite energy means finite computation.

But *not infinite* is not the same as scarce.

There is still a serious problem with this answer: another star buys more computation. A civilization that maximizes total lives, subjective years, simulations, redundancy, knowledge, or survival probability should take every star it can reach. Digital minds make colonization easier, not harder. A seed machine can travel for ten thousand years, build a computer, and wake a copy that experienced no journey at all.

The paradox also does not require every civilization to expand. It requires one civilization, one faction, or one badly aligned machine to do it once.

Virtual reality therefore does not solve the Fermi paradox by itself. It moves the question from propulsion to utility functions.

**Why does the marginal star eventually stop being worth taking?**

The stronger answer is that mature civilizations converge on bounded goals. They do not maximize computation forever. They acquire enough energy to make experience cheap, choose a population and a purpose, and then optimize for stability instead of scale. Uncontrolled copies are treated like cancers. Distant colonies are treated like irreversible goal drift.

Biological life cannot easily make this choice. Evolution rewards whatever keeps reproducing, not whatever decides it has enough. A postbiological civilization with stable values can end that selection pressure. Its greatest engineering project is not building a fleet. It is preventing the process that would eventually build one.

This sounds less like a society and more like a single mature machine. That may be exactly what survives.

It would not be completely invisible. Computation consumes energy and produces waste heat. Infrared searches already constrain the most extravagant galaxy-scale civilizations, and searches of millions of nearby stars have begun looking for partial Dyson swarms.[^5] But the broader technosignature search remains tiny, and a civilization with bounded energy use could be much quieter than the empires imagined by Kardashev.[^6]

The most likely answer to the Fermi paradox is probably multiplication rather than one clean mechanism: intelligent life is rarer than optimistic Drake equations imply, technological civilizations overlap poorly in time, our searches are primitive, and the survivors become quiet. Realistic uncertainty in the biological terms alone leaves substantial probability that nobody else is close.[^7]

Simulation is not the whole answer. It is the best explanation for the final term: why a civilization that survives might stop looking like an empire.

The physical universe is slow, sparse, and mostly empty. A simulated universe can be immediate, dense, and built around whatever its inhabitants actually value. Once a civilization can choose between exploring the universe it inherited and creating universes it prefers, the second option wins.

The galaxy may look empty for the same reason a server room looks empty.

Almost everything that matters is happening inside.
