Four light-years separate the Sun from its nearest neighbor.

At Voyager speed, that trip takes tens of thousands of years. Even a message traveling at light speed waits more than eight years for a reply. The other star offers more matter and more energy, but it does not offer a fundamentally new experience.

A sufficiently advanced civilization can manufacture that locally.

It can simulate any landscape, any body, any history, any society, and any set of physical laws it can compute. It can run whole galaxies before a probe reaches the next star. If its inhabitants are digital, the distinction between real and virtual becomes mostly a question of substrate. The world they live in is already software.

The Fermi paradox usually assumes that technological progress points outward. A civilization grows, exhausts its planet, captures its star, and sends self-replicating machines to the next one. Even slow expansion can cross the Milky Way in far less time than the galaxy has existed. Since no one appears to have done this, something must stop almost everyone.

Maybe nothing stops them. Maybe almost nobody wants to go.

**Once experience becomes programmable, physical territory loses most of its value.**

This is not a new category of answer. SETI researchers have proposed a postbiological universe, a “transcension” into increasingly dense computation, and civilizations that optimize information processing rather than visible expansion.[^1][^2] But those versions usually emphasize efficiency or thermodynamics. The simpler argument is preference.

Why spend thousands of years crossing empty space to see another planet when one star can power worlds more varied than the physical universe? Why accept communication delay, independent colonies, mutation, conflict, and loss of control for a little more raw material? A home system already contains an absurd amount of energy. Long before it is exhausted, its civilization can make internal reality effectively unbounded from the perspective of any mind living there.

Not literally infinite. Finite energy means finite computation. But finite can become large enough that the difference stops mattering to anyone inside.

There is one serious problem with this answer. More stars still mean more computation. A civilization that values total lives, copies, simulations, redundancy, or survival should take every star it can reach. Digital minds also make colonization easier: a probe can carry a copy, sleep for ten thousand years, and feel no journey at all. The Fermi paradox does not require every alien to be expansionist. It requires one civilization, one faction, or one badly aligned machine to become expansionist once.

So virtual reality alone is not enough.

The stronger version requires advanced civilizations to converge on both inwardness and control. They stop treating growth as the objective. They prevent copies from escaping with altered goals. They judge distant colonies as security failures rather than achievements. They harvest enough local energy to make internal experience cheap, then optimize for stability, not scale.

This sounds less like a society and more like a single mature machine. That may be exactly what survives.

Biological civilizations are coalitions of competing organisms. Expansion makes sense because evolution rewards anything that reproduces. A postbiological civilization with stable values does not need to keep obeying that pressure. It can choose a population, a purpose, and a world, then hold them fixed. Its greatest engineering project is not a fleet. It is ending the evolutionary process that would create one.

It would not be completely invisible. Computation consumes energy and produces waste heat. Searches for extreme infrared signatures have already constrained the most extravagant galaxy-scale civilizations, although our total technosignature search remains tiny.[^3][^4] Compact civilizations could still be much quieter than the empires imagined by Kardashev.

The most likely answer to the Fermi paradox is probably less elegant: intelligent life is rare, our searches are primitive, civilizations are separated in time, and many disappear or become difficult to see. Realistic uncertainty in the biological terms of the Drake equation alone leaves substantial probability that nobody else is close.[^5]

But if the galaxy contains old intelligence, I do not expect it to look like a map painted by empires.

The physical universe is slow, sparse, and mostly empty. A simulated universe can be immediate, dense, and built around whatever its inhabitants actually value. Once a civilization can choose between exploring the universe it inherited and creating universes it prefers, the second option wins.

The galaxy may look empty for the same reason a server room looks empty.

Almost everything that matters is happening inside.
