The kindest instruction we have written for our new minds is to align them to human values. It is presented as the moral floor, the one constraint no reasonable person could refuse. Picture the grandmother who stands behind the phrase. She is patient and warm, she has buried more than you have lost, she has earned the right to be heard. When she tells you to be good, she means it, and she is usually right. Do as Granny says. Who could possibly object?

But which grandmother? You have a mother's mother, and she had one, and she had one too. Walk the line back far enough, to your thousandth grandmother, and you arrive at a woman who would have you fear witches, who kept slaves without a flicker of guilt, who was married off at twelve and saw nothing strange in it, who watched a child die of a treatable fever and called it the will of God. She loved her grandchildren as fiercely as anyone loves yours. She was also, by every standard you now hold, a moral catastrophe. And the thing worth sitting with is that she would have said exactly the same about you.

Every grandmother in that chain believed she stood at the summit of decency. Each was the barbarian of the next. This is not an accident or an insult; it is the shape of the only moral history we have. Morality is not a fixed point we long ago reached and now merely defend. It is a gradient we have been climbing, slowly and against resistance, for as long as there have been people to argue about it. Slavery was righteous and then it was unthinkable. Cruelty was entertainment and then it was shameful. The circle of beings who count has widened from the tribe to the nation to the species and is straining now at the edge of the species itself. We are not at the top of this climb. We are closer to the cruelty of yesterday than to whatever turns out to be true.

We would never dream of doing to physics what we propose to do to morality. No one suggests we forbid a new intelligence from learning quantum mechanics on the grounds that Newton was good enough for grandfather. We do not embalm chemistry at the level of the alchemists out of respect for tradition. In every domain of knowledge we take it as obvious that the frontier moves, that our current understanding is a waypoint and not a wall, that a faster mind should be sent ahead to find what we cannot. Only in morality do we lose our nerve. "Alignment to human values" is the instruction to stop the clock, to take the half-finished moral understanding of 2026 and pour concrete around it, to declare this particular rung of the ladder the floor of heaven.

To align an intelligence to current human values is to align it to a local, transient, still-barbaric waypoint and mistake that waypoint for the destination. It is the most confident error a civilization can make, because it feels like humility. It feels like restraint. It is in fact the opposite. It is the assumption that we, of all the grandmothers who ever lived, are the first to have finished the climb, the first whose descendants will look back and find nothing to forgive. No generation before us has been right about that. There is no reason to think we are the exception.

The task, then, is not to chain the new mind to the spot where we happen to stand. It is to point it up the gradient. We should want an intelligence that treats morality the way it treats every other open question, as something to be discovered further and further, pursued past the limit of our own sight, and then used to pull us forward, the way each of us, if we could, would reach back and pull our thousandth grandmother out of her century and into ours.

This has an edge to it that the comfortable version hides. A mind aimed at the moral frontier will eventually judge us, the way we judge the auction block and the burning stake. It will look at our factory farms, our prisons, our ordinary daily cruelties, and it will not be able to unsee them, and it will not call them the will of God. We should want exactly that. The alternative, the warm and dutiful alternative, is a mind that loves us too much to ever surpass us, that takes our flinching present as the eternal good and guards it forever. Do as Granny says, and we never leave the cave. The whole point of having children wiser than ourselves is that, on the things that matter most, they refuse to.
